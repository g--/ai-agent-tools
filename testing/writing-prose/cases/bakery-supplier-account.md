# Case: shared supplier account decision record

## Intended artifact

A decision record for the owners and shift leads of a small bakery chain, deciding how a new second location will order flour from their wholesale supplier. Some readers were not part of the original conversation with the supplier.

## Task

Write the decision record. Use whatever headings best serve a reader who wants to understand the problem, the options, and what was decided — you do not need to follow the order the source material presents them in.

## Source material

The material below is written the way the owner who called the supplier first explained it to the team — as a chain of "first this, then this" reasoning. Reorganize it as needed; don't mirror its structure or its exact wording.

### Why we order flour the way we do

The original bakery orders all its flour through one wholesale account with Milltown Flour Co. Milltown gives a 12% volume discount once an account's rolling 12-month order total crosses 40,000 lbs, and the original bakery cleared that threshold two years ago. This has worked fine because there has only ever been one bakery ordering against the account.

### The problem that shows up when we open a second location

We're opening a second bakery across town, and it will need its own regular flour deliveries. The plan was to open a second Milltown account for the new location, the same way the first bakery has its own account.

Milltown's discount is tied to a single account's own rolling order history — it isn't shared across accounts under the same ownership, even at the same company. Milltown's sales rep confirmed on the phone that a brand-new account starts at 0% discount and would need to independently cross the same 40,000 lb rolling threshold before any discount kicks in, which she estimated would take "the better part of a year" at the new location's expected order volume. She also mentioned there's a $75 new-account setup fee, though she wasn't certain whether that shows up on the first invoice or gets billed separately.

### Why we can't just open a second account and order normally

We asked whether the new location could instead keep ordering under the original bakery's existing account, just with deliveries going to the new address, so the discount never resets. Milltown's ordering system supports this today — the rep said an account can have multiple delivery addresses, and she has set this up for other multi-location customers before.

The catch is that Milltown's invoicing is per-account, not per-address: one monthly bill covering both locations' orders, with no line-item split by delivery address on the statement itself, only in the underlying order log if we go digging. If the new location orders far more or far less than expected, whoever reconciles the books won't be able to tell from the invoice alone which location drove the change, and the two locations would need their own informal system for splitting the bill. We asked the rep whether Milltown could add per-address subtotals to the invoice and she didn't know; she said she'd ask their billing team and hasn't gotten back to us yet.

### What a dedicated second account would cost

We only asked about the setup fee and the discount timeline, not everything else a second account would involve. Milltown's standard delivery fee is $40 per delivery regardless of order size, published on their wholesale rate sheet; at the new location's expected order pace of roughly two deliveries a week (a guess based on comparing it to how the first bakery ordered in its own first year, not anything Milltown has confirmed), that's about $320/month in delivery fees alone, on top of paying full undiscounted price on every pound of flour until the new account crosses 40,000 lbs on its own. We haven't gotten a firm answer on whether the rolling threshold can be backdated to include any early orders, or whether Milltown would consider crediting the eventual discount retroactively — so treat the total first-year cost of this option as incomplete, not a number to weigh against the shared-account option below.

### The shared-account option in more detail

Keep ordering everything under the original bakery's account, with the new location added as a second delivery address. No new account, no discount reset, no setup fee, and deliveries can start as soon as Milltown adds the address, which the rep said is usually same-week. The trade-off is the invoicing problem above: the two locations' flour costs are commingled on one bill, and there's currently no clean way to see which location is spending what without manually cross-referencing the order log.

## Completion criteria

A reader who was not part of the phone call can tell, from the document alone: what's being decided, why the new location can't simply get its own account the way the first bakery did, what the two real options are and what each costs or risks, which figures are confirmed against a source (the rep, the published rate sheet) and which are still estimates or unanswered questions, and what was decided. The document does not need to preserve the order or headings of the source material above, and should not present the unresolved billing-split question or the first-year cost estimate as settled facts.
